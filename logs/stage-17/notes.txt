STAGE 17 — THE INVERTED INDEX AND BM25
======================================

GOAL
----
Answer the question the project has been circling: why is search lightning
fast? Not because the crawler is quick. Because the crawling already happened,
and query time is a lookup in a structure built offline.

Build that structure, score with BM25, and MEASURE it against the obvious
alternative so the answer is a number rather than an assertion.


STEPS TAKEN
-----------
1. minicrawl/tokenize.py — case-folding, NFKC, Unicode-aware splitting.
2. minicrawl/index.py — postings, BM25, save/load, add_jsonl.
3. minicrawl-search CLI: --build from a stage-15 export, then query.
4. scripts/search_bench.py — inverted index vs forward scan, same scoring,
   three corpus sizes, plus a selectivity breakdown.
5. Rebuilt the benchmark corpus after the first one made the result
   meaningless (see WHAT BROKE).
6. 27 tests.


CONCEPTS APPLIED
----------------
- Inverted index: term -> documents, so a query reads only the postings of its
  own terms and never looks at the rest of the corpus.
- Query cost tracks postings length, i.e. term rarity — not corpus size.
- BM25: saturation (k1), IDF, length normalisation (b).
- The +1 IDF variant: the classic formula goes negative for terms in >50% of
  documents, letting a document rank LOWER for containing a query word.
- Why no stopword list: BM25 already discounts them, and removing them breaks
  phrase queries.
- Tokenisation decides findability: a discarded term is unmatchable forever.
- NFKC: ligatures and compatibility forms must fold to what people type.
- Zipf's law is why inverted indexes work; a corpus without it tests nothing.
- Structural equivalence: a faster structure must return identical answers.

WHAT BROKE
----------
1. THE BENCHMARK CORPUS MADE THE MEASUREMENT MEANINGLESS.
   The first version built documents by resampling sixteen sentences. Every
   term then appeared in about a third of all documents, so postings lists
   were nearly as long as the corpus and traversing them cost roughly what
   scanning everything cost. Measured speedup: 2x.

   Worse, I had already written the summary line "cost tracks how rare the
   words are, not how big the corpus is" — which the table directly
   contradicted, since latency grew from 228us to 12,244us with corpus size.
   I nearly shipped a conclusion my own numbers disproved.

   Real text is Zipfian, and that distribution is the whole reason an inverted
   index works. Rebuilt on a 20,000-word vocabulary sampled by rank:
   speedup went 2x -> 61x at 50,000 documents, and the claim became true.

   This is stage 6 for the fourth time: a degenerate corpus tolerating a wrong
   conclusion. The rule holds — if the corpus tolerates it, the corpus is
   wrong. The benchmark now states its own construction in its docstring,
   because a synthetic corpus can be built to prove anything.

2. THE HEADLINE WOULD HAVE BEEN A HALF-TRUTH ANYWAY.
   Even with a good corpus, 61x is the best case. A term present in 49,990 of
   50,000 documents costs 24,591us against a scan's 31,240us — no real gain,
   because its postings list IS the corpus. Reported as its own row rather
   than averaged away.


MEASURED
--------
  documents   build    index p50   scan p50   speedup
      1,000   0.03s        15us      216us       14x
     10,000   0.36s        92us    3,011us       33x
     50,000   2.00s       509us   31,240us       61x

  selectivity, 50,000 documents:
     36 postings         13us
  2,289 postings        503us
 49,990 postings     24,591us   <- almost exactly the cost of a scan


5. CROSS-VALIDATED AGAINST AN IMPLEMENTATION I DID NOT WRITE (added later).
   The brute-force scan the index was checked against lives in this repo and
   came from the same reading of the formula, so agreement between them proves
   consistency, not correctness. rank_bm25 is independent.

   Result: scores identical to 1.8e-15 over 13,584 scored documents; 300 of 300
   multi-term rankings identical; 400 of 400 SINGLE-TERM rankings identical
   with our code unmodified — the decisive one, because IDF cannot reorder a
   single-term query, which isolates saturation and length normalisation.

   Two differences, both parameters:
     k1   rank_bm25 defaults to 1.5; we use Lucene's 1.2.
     idf  rank_bm25 uses the classic formula with a floor for negative values;
          we use Lucene's log(1 + ...), which cannot go negative.

   MY OWN ERRORS ALONG THE WAY, BOTH THE SAME SHAPE AS EARLIER STAGES:
     - First comparison ran on an 18-word vocabulary over 200 documents, where
       nearly every term appears in half the corpus. It reported "400 of 400
       explained" — a condition that could not have failed. Stage 17's own
       benchmark mistake, repeated while validating stage 17.
     - Saw a 0.46 score gap and wrote "the scoring functions genuinely differ"
       before decomposing it. It was rank_bm25's default k1.

  uv run python -m testsite.server --ports 8081 &

      --export pages.jsonl

STATE AFTER THIS STAGE
----------------------
- A crawl can be indexed and queried in microseconds.
- The claim "search is fast" is now a table, with its caveat next to it.
- Still absent: positions (so no phrase queries), stemming, a seekable on-disk
  index, an HTTP search endpoint, and tuned k1/b (which needs judged data).
